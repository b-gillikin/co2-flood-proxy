# Limburg AI ideas (internal research notes)

These are candidate directions, not additions to the live chapter design or dissertation prose. Brian writes the manuscript text.

## Idea 1 — Uneven tributary response to the same storm

Test whether a modest nonlinear model can identify which of the six monitored tributaries will respond unusually strongly, using rainfall and river state already observed at a defined decision time. Compare with persistence and the existing logistic baseline on whole held-out storms and years. The scientific target is within-storm response heterogeneity, not the generic result that rain precedes high water. This idea has not been run. See `limburg-retrospective-prediction-test-2026-10-03.md` and `limburg-atmospheric-increment-2026-10-03.md` for relevant baselines.

## Idea 2 — An evaluation of basin-grounded AI reasoning

**Candidate research question.** Does grounding AI in river-basin structure and data provenance make its assessments of Limburg high-water events more reliable?

**Core experiment.** Give four systems the same historical high-water questions: (1) a general LLM, (2) document-only RAG, (3) RAG plus checked gauge-data tools but without the curated river map, and (4) RAG with both the checked tools and curated river/agency map. Score whether each identifies river relationships, applicable agency rules, measurement problems, and what cannot be concluded. The third arm helps distinguish the contribution of checked data from the additional contribution of basin structure. Use the same underlying document corpus for arms 2–4 and record exactly what each arm can access. This is a candidate RQ for a potential later study, not a replacement for the live Limburg chapter RQs. The targeted literature scan and its limits are in `limburg-ai-idea-2-literature-scan-2026-10-04.md`.

**Initial geography.** The Dutch Limburg Meuse reach, the six monitored tributaries, and a focused Netherlands–Germany comparison around the Wurm/Worm and directly relevant German tributary records. Belgian Meuse information can supply context, but the held data do not support a symmetric three-country evaluation. This is not a system for the entire international Maas basin or every national FEWS.

**Architecture.**

1. A curated relational/graph layer stores station identity, river and catchment membership, upstream/downstream links, responsible agency, measurement type, rating-era and known quality metadata, each with provenance and validity dates. A graph database is optional; ordinary tables are sufficient initially.
2. A versioned document store holds official agency manuals, warning conventions, reports, and station notes, indexed by source, jurisdiction, publication/effective date, and cited passage. Research papers such as Busker et al. explain the problem and help formulate questions; they do not supersede primary agency rules.
3. Deterministic tools read rainfall and discharge, align timestamps, compute trends, apply source quality codes and rating-domain checks, and answer topological queries. Numerical and geographic facts are computed here, not inferred by a language model.
4. Hybrid exact-term/semantic retrieval supplies relevant passages. A general generative model selects tools and synthesizes a structured, cited evidence brief. A checker rejects unsupported numbers, wrong-period rules, incorrect upstream/downstream statements, and unsupported equivalence between countries' warning colours. Unknown or unavailable information is reported as such.

This is **RAG plus structured basin facts and tools**, rather than a bare chatbot over PDFs. No fine-tuning is part of the initial design. Fine-tuning could later be tested for a narrow, repeatable extraction or formatting task if evaluation exposes a stable failure; the model would still retrieve current facts at answer time.

**Concrete test case.** For a July 2021 Venlo question, the system should find that much of the held high-water discharge series carries RWS code `25` (interpolated in space and time), cite the source record, and distinguish this from direct measurement. For a historical Waterschap Fase-threshold question, it should not apply today's threshold to 2021 when the provider has said historical thresholds are unavailable. The system should describe what the evidence supports and what needs human verification; it should not issue an automated public warning.

**Evaluation evidence is separate from the corpus.** Construct hand-adjudicated, source-linked answers for historical event questions. Compare the four arms on factual correctness, missed quality flags, wrong jurisdiction or topology, temporal/version errors, unsupported claims, justified abstention, and time to assemble an answer. Hold the question set fixed, use the same document corpus for the retrieval arms, and document exactly which sources and tools each system can access. Include hard but realistic cases: similar station names on different rivers; a current rule retrieved for a historical event; inconsistent agency descriptions; a reconstructed, interpolated, or provisional value; and an unsupported question for which abstention is correct. Distinguish wrong assertions from unnecessary refusals. Documents and observations are source evidence; the independently checked answers are the evaluation reference. A retrospective archive test can establish situation-assessment quality, not live warning effectiveness. An operational shadow test would later need as-issued data and current agency interfaces.

**Design lesson from [HydroAgent (2026 preprint)](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.23983).** Keep the LLM's event interpretation separate from deterministic calculations and from the output contract. For this assessment task, a compact contract could require event and station identity, retrieved source passages, computed observations with quality flags, claims with supporting source IDs, unresolved conflicts, and an explicit `insufficient_evidence` field. Log tool calls and intermediate artifacts so a wrong answer can be traced to retrieval, river identity, calculation, or synthesis. Test whether one erroneous early judgment contaminates the final assessment. HydroAgent uses a staged forecasting and hydrological-model workflow with expert review; its model calibration, analog-based peak/volume judgments, rolling forecasts, and warning publication are not required for this retrospective study. Its full live execution environment is not publicly released, so this is design inspiration, not a replication claim.

**Possible later comparator.** A deterministic rules-and-template report could test whether generative synthesis adds value over checked facts and fixed language. This is optional after the core experiment, not another required arm. A second LLM could check whether results depend strongly on one model, again only if the initial results make that worthwhile.

**Model and retrieval choices to examine, without multiplying the core arms.** In the architecture comparison, freeze one capable general-purpose text LLM, its version, prompt, and decoding settings. “General LLM” means this same model answering without retrieval; it is not a claim about all LLMs. After the core test, rerun a small diagnostic subset with an available hydrology-adapted open model to ask whether domain training changes the benefit of basin grounding; compare under identical evidence access and report the model's actual training/evaluation domain. [HydroLLM](https://doi.org/10.2166/hydro.2025.100) is a relevant candidate, but its published evaluation is hydrology knowledge QA, not Dutch–German agency-rule or gauge-quality assessment. Avoid a model sweep until the task and error categories are stable.

For document retrieval, start with a credible hybrid of exact-name/code search and semantic search, retaining page/passage IDs, language, agency, publication date, and effective date. Exact matching matters for station IDs and official terms; semantic matching helps across wording and languages. Diagnose retrieval separately from final answer quality: did the system retrieve the correct document and passage before it reasoned incorrectly? [HydroEmbed](https://doi.org/10.2166/wst.2025.156) can be a later retrieval challenger, but its reported tests use hydrology textbook QA, so it must earn its place on German/Dutch local records. A geography/time-aware applicability check is a promising variant when errors show that retrieved text is about the wrong river, agency, or year; [GeoRisk-RAG](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22634) is a related preprint on geographic validity and selective answering in wildfire QA. A full auto-extracted GraphRAG database is not assumed necessary; the small curated river map and source metadata are the scientific inputs here.

Earth-observation foundation models such as [Prithvi-EO-2.0](https://doi.org/10.1109/TGRS.2025.3642610) and river-forecast models such as [GraphRiverCast](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22293) process imagery or hydrodynamic forcings for mapping/forecasting tasks. They do not directly answer this source-provenance question. They may belong to a separate mapping or forecasting experiment if that becomes scientifically motivated, rather than being added to the Idea 2 comparator set.

**Output and interface.** The research output is a source-linked situation assessment, not an automatically issued warning. A simple page could let a reader select an event and inspect the assessment, supporting passages, quality flags, and underlying records. It need not be a full FEWS dashboard. The UI presents the experiment and its evidence; it is not the research contribution by itself.

**Netherlands–Germany comparison within the same experiment.** Test three matched task sets: Dutch-only assessments, German-only assessments, and cross-border assessments that must reconcile both sources. The focal cross-border case is the Wurm/Worm: LANUK's delivered packages include Herzogenrath 1 and Randerath on the Wurm, while Waterschap Limburg supplied Worm/Rimburg. Verify exact gauge order and overlapping usable periods before constructing event cases. LANUK's Honsdorf and Herzogenrath 2 belong to Beeckfließ and Broicher Bach, respectively; the system should not relabel them as Wurm gauges. Compare the same AI architectures across task sets and ask whether country-aware source selection, time conventions, rating/quality provenance and river topology reduce errors when evidence crosses the border. The scope is an assessment of the selected NL/DE records and agencies, not a ranking of national warning-system performance. Historical Waterschap Fase thresholds are unavailable, and LANUK reconstruction/provisional-curve details are still being clarified; correct abstention is part of the test where records cannot support a historical threshold or exact-flow claim.

**Why this matters.** The [2026 comparison of northwestern European FEWS](https://nhess.copernicus.org/articles/26/1457/2026/nhess-26-1457-2026.html) reports differing warning levels and codes, challenges translating forecasts into actions, and a specific gap in cross-border warning exchange even where data exchange is relatively well organized. The [international Meuse flood-risk plan](https://www.meuse-maas.be/CIM/media/PGRI-dec-2021-en-anglais/Roof_Report_Mregie_21_1def_en.pdf) documents different institutional roles and message contents. Flood knowledge graphs and flood RAG systems already exist, including [a 2025 flood-KG/RAG evaluation](https://www.mdpi.com/1999-4893/18/11/713). Similarity to that work is not disqualifying: the contribution can come from a different, well-evaluated task and setting, careful use of Dutch–German river relationships and measurement provenance, and a clearer account of when the AI should refrain from a conclusion. These are claims to test, not assumed results.
